# Heat, harm, and what to do about it — in plain language

*A short version of the full analysis, written for anyone. No background needed. Every technical term is explained the first time it appears. Sources are listed at the end.*

---

## 1. What causes the most harm

**Heat kills more people in Europe than any other weather event, and it kills them quietly.**

Not in dramatic ways. Mostly it is an older person, living alone, in a flat that got hot and stayed hot for several nights in a row. Their heart and circulation are working overtime to shed heat. They never get a cool night to recover. After three or four nights, something gives. The death certificate usually says heart failure or stroke, not "heat."

This is why heat deaths are easy to miss. They are spread across thousands of homes, they are recorded under other causes, and they are counted months later in mortality statistics rather than reported as they happen.

**The scale is large and getting larger.** News reports in August 2026 put Europe's excess summer deaths above 30,000 for that summer, with Spain recording its worst heat year to date. EU health advisers warned in July 2026, after roughly 16,000 excess deaths, that the response is still reactive rather than prepared.

### The three things that matter most, in order

1. **Hot nights, not hot days.** A hot day is survivable if the night cools down. The body repairs itself overnight. When the night stays above about 20 °C, that repair does not happen — and when that repeats for several nights, risk climbs sharply. Our measurements show hot nights have increased in every single city we looked at.
2. **Indoor temperature, not outdoor temperature.** People die inside buildings. A poorly insulated top-floor flat can stay hot for many hours after the outside air has cooled. The weather forecast can say the heatwave has broken while the bedroom has not.
3. **Being alone.** Someone who lives alone has nobody to notice they are struggling. This is the single strongest risk factor that is not about temperature at all.

## 2. The main finding, in one sentence

**We are very good at measuring the weather and very bad at checking whether anyone was actually helped.**

Think of it as a chain running from "the weather turns hot" to "a person dies." We watch the first two links closely and the last one late. The three links in the middle — did the warning reach them, did their home get hot, did it stay hot all night — we barely watch at all.

![How heat harms people, and where our monitoring stops](../figures/how_heat_harms.png)

The left side of the picture is the whole argument. **Green means we watch it well. Red means we are not watching.** The red band sits exactly where a person could still be helped.

This matters because you cannot fix what you do not measure. We know how many people died. We do not know how many of them got a warning, whether they understood it, whether anyone checked on them, or how hot their bedroom was. So we cannot tell which part of the response failed, and we keep repeating the same approach each summer.

The same pattern shows up on the emissions side. Satellites can now spot a large methane leak from space and pinpoint which facility it came from. What generally does not follow is a public record of whether it was fixed. There is some progress: a UN satellite alert programme reports more than 40 large leaks curbed since 2023. That is real, and it is also a small number against the scale of the problem.

## 3. What we measured ourselves

We took 76 years of temperature records (1950–2025) for twelve southern European cities and counted how often nights are too warm for the body to recover.

**Hot nights rose in every city, without exception.** Athens went from 61 such nights a year to 83. Rome nearly tripled. Madrid more than tripled.

**The most striking number is how many hot nights come in a row.** This is the one that matters physiologically, because it measures how many nights pass with no chance to recover:

| City | Then (1961–1990) | Now (1996–2025) |
|---|---|---|
| Valletta | 89 nights in a row | **105** |
| Palermo | 38 | **54** |
| Athens | 24 | **44** |
| Rome | 5 | **15** |

**No heat warning system in Europe currently uses this as a trigger.** They trigger on how hot the day will be.

**One honest correction.** We expected to find nights warming faster than days. They are not — summer days are warming faster (0.35 °C per decade versus 0.23 °C for nights, with days faster in 11 of the 12 cities). We are reporting this because it is what the data says. It does not change the conclusion: nights matter more because they start much closer to the dangerous threshold, so even slower warming pushes far more nights across the line.

## 4. What to do, easiest first

Ranked by how hard it is to *start*, not by cost. Several of the cheapest things are among the hardest to begin, because they need an agreement rather than a budget.

### Start this month — no permission, no equipment, no budget

**Run heavy computing and charging when the grid is clean.** Things that can wait — large computing jobs, electric car charging, water heating, cooling a building early in the morning — can be shifted to hours when electricity is cleanest. Free data on this already exists. *Nothing gets done less; it just gets done at a different hour.* This is the clearest example of cutting emissions without slowing technology down at all. Takes about two weeks to show a result.

**Map the shady walking routes.** Free satellite imagery and open maps can work out which streets are shaded, and the result goes into the city's existing map app. It is a software job on data that is already public. Useful for anyone who has to walk somewhere in the afternoon and has no car.

### Needs one agreement

**Tell people exactly when to open their windows.** Opening windows at the right hour and closing them at the wrong one makes a real difference to how hot a flat gets overnight. Send it by text message so it works without a smartphone. Costs well under a euro per person per year.

**Put a cheap sensor in the bedroom of someone at risk — and have it call somebody.** A sensor costing under €30. If the bedroom stays too warm for two nights running, the system triggers *an actual phone call from the council*, not a notification on a phone the person may not own. **The phone call is the part that does not exist anywhere at scale today.** Everything else is off-the-shelf. Around €6 per person per year.

**Measure whether cooling projects worked.** Put aside 1–2% of any publicly funded cooling or tree-planting budget to measure temperature before and after, and publish it. Costs almost nothing and is the only way we ever learn which measures are worth repeating.

**Open cool public rooms, and count who comes.** Libraries and sports halls are already air-conditioned and already paid for. Publish their opening hours with the heat warning, and put a €20 counter on the door so you know whether anyone actually came. One honest limitation: these close at night, which is when the risk is highest.

### Needs a budget and someone whose job it is

**Put sensors on buses and bin lorries.** A city's existing vehicles cover more streets in a day than any affordable fixed network. Roughly half a cent per resident per year.

**Set up a calibration service.** Thousands of cheap weather sensors already exist across Europe, bought by schools, councils and hobbyists. They are mostly ignored by officials because nobody knows how accurate they are. A regional station where any sensor can be checked for two weeks would turn all of them into usable public data. **This is the best value in the whole list** — about 4 cents per person per year — and it is hard only because it needs a permanent home.

**Agree a common data format.** So each city's measurements can be read by everyone else, instead of every city building its own incompatible system.

**Use electricity meter data to find people with no cooling.** Smart meters already record usage every 15 minutes. A home using no cooling at all during a heatwave, where an elderly person lives, is a home worth visiting. No new equipment is needed — but the privacy agreement takes months, so this is the one to start negotiating first even though it finishes last.

### Bigger programme

**Fit external shutters and blinds before anything else.** Shading on the *outside* of a window stops heat before it gets in; blinds on the inside do not. Far cheaper than full insulation work or handing out air conditioners, and it works without electricity.

## 6. What we are not sure about

**The cost figures are estimates, not quotes.** They come from component prices and reasonable assumptions about how many people each thing covers. Trust them to tell you that one option costs a hundred times less than another. Do not trust them to tell you that €6 is better than €8.

**The ease rankings are judgements.** In your particular town, a privacy agreement might take a week or might be impossible. Re-check locally.

**The death figures for summer 2026 are early estimates** reported in the press, and they will be revised.

**One part of this update is missing.** We intended to refresh the academic publication counts through 2026, but the database key was not approved for this session, so those figures still reflect the earlier pull. Nothing in the findings above depends on them.

**What would prove us wrong.** If a city with good sensors and a mature heat plan still showed no preventable deaths, our central claim — that the problem is acting on information rather than collecting it — would be wrong. The evidence so far points the other way: plans are widespread and people keep dying. The indoor-sensor idea is the easiest to test: fit a few hundred homes and check whether bedroom temperature predicts illness better than the outdoor forecast does. If it does not, drop that idea and keep the rest.

---

**A note on health.** This describes risk across whole populations and how to monitor it. It is not medical advice. If you are worried about a specific person's health in hot weather, that is a conversation for a doctor or nurse who knows their situation — not something a temperature reading can settle.
