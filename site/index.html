1. What is being monitored

Forty-six operational systems were inventoried across eight layers, each scored on resolution, latency, openness, funding model, and — the column that turned out to matter most — whether the output is wired to any actuator that changes a physical outcome without a human choosing to act.

The split is the finding. Space-based greenhouse-gas observation and urban environmental sensing — 18 systems between them — trigger nothing at all. The layers that do act (regulatory reporting, grid telemetry, hazard warning) act because a regulation or a market obliges them to, not because the sensing is better.

3. How it changed

Daily maximum and minimum temperatures were pulled from ERA5 reanalysis for twelve southern European cities over 1950–2025 (333,108 city-days), and indicators computed per city-year.

3.1 The extremes moved faster than the averages

Between the 1960s and the 2020s, nights above 20 °C rose 1.5× while nights above 25 °C rose 6.5× and days above 35 °C 2.7×. The further into the tail you look, the faster the growth — which is why averages understate the change people actually feel. The 2020s row covers 2020–2025 only.

3.3 The full trend record

A result that contradicted our expectation. We set out expecting nights to be warming faster than days. They are not: summer maxima rise at 0.35 °C/decade against 0.23 for minima, with days faster in 11 of 12 cities. We report it as found. The case for focusing on nights survives on distribution rather than rate — night minima sit close to the threshold at which the body cannot recover, so the slower trend crosses that line on far more nights than the faster daytime trend crosses 40 °C.

4. What we can do

Twelve interventions, each specified with capital and running cost, deploying institution, expected effect, evidence strength, and explicit failure modes. All twelve are compatible with continued technological development; one of them consists of running more computation, scheduled differently.

6. Problems, limits, and what would prove this wrong

Problems in the evidence

  • Reanalysis is modelled, not observed. ERA5 assimilates observations into a physical model on a grid of roughly 30 km. It represents the climate signal at a city's location well; it does not represent the street the person lives on, and it cannot represent their bedroom.
  • Thresholds are conventions. 20 °C for a warm night and 35 °C for a hot day are the standard European definitions, not physiological constants. Risk is continuous, and the real threshold varies with humidity, age, medication, and acclimatisation.
  • Summer-2026 mortality figures are early press estimates and will be revised as official statistics catch up. They agree on order of magnitude, which is the only claim made from them here.
  • Publication counts measure attention, not knowledge. A theme growing from 7 papers to 209 reflects a term entering circulation as much as a field maturing.
  • The inventory is a considered sample, not a census. Forty-six systems chosen for coverage across layers; the actuator-coupling score is a judgement applied consistently, not a measured quantity.
  • Ease scores weight four components equally. In any specific municipality a data-protection agreement may be trivial or fatal. Re-score locally before committing.

What would falsify the central claim

The argument is that the binding constraint is coupling — acting on information — rather than sensing. It would be wrong if a well-instrumented city with a mature heat plan showed no residual preventable mortality. Current European evidence points the other way: heat plans are widespread and excess deaths persist through recent summers.

The cheapest falsifiable piece is the indoor-sensor claim. Deploy a few hundred bedroom sensors and test whether indoor night minima predict individual health events better than the outdoor forecast that currently drives warnings. If they do not, the household tier of the catalogue collapses and the cross-cutting tier still stands.

On health. This report concerns population-level risk and monitoring design. It is not clinical guidance. Decisions about any individual's care — including anyone a system of this kind flags as at risk — rest with qualified health professionals who have the full context.
